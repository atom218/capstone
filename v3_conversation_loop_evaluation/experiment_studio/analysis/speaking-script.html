Opening — about 1 minute

The encouraging finding is that emotional and behavioural grounding can improve specific parts of a synthetic-user answer. I’ll show five selected examples, starting with the clearest differences, and name exactly which evaluation criteria improve.

There are three criteria to keep in mind. Specificity means the answer gives a concrete, precisely scoped requirement. User consequence means it explains an effect on a person and the condition that makes it matter. Product insight means it supports a focused product decision or question to investigate.

Each example shows the same question, the full baseline and grounded answers, and both judges’ scores. The green cells show actual score increases. Grey cells show ties. These are scores for individual answers on a zero-to-three scale, rather than averages across an interview.

The first example has an improvement under both judges. In the other four, Claude records the improvement and GPT gives equal scores. I’ll keep that distinction explicit.

1. Explain information use before requesting disclosure — about 2 minutes

This is a Mental Wellness example using C2 detailed grounding. The question asks what the user would do if none of the available check-in labels felt right, and what would make sharing more information worthwhile.

The baseline is already sensible. It says the user would choose the closest label, appreciate space to explain, and expect personalisation in return.

The grounded answer adds a specific requirement: tell me upfront how the extra information will be used, before asking me to provide it.

Claude’s specificity score increases from two to three because the requirement is tied to a precise point in the interaction. Its product-insight score also increases from two to three because the answer supports a focused design decision: put the explanation at the moment the app requests more context.

GPT records a different improvement. Its user-consequence score increases from two to three because willingness to disclose depends on knowing the use and practical value of the information.

So both judges find an improvement, but on different criteria. This is the clearest shared example in the selected collection. The extra value is the timing of the explanation, rather than a broad request for better personalisation.

[Pause to point to Claude’s two green cells and GPT’s user-consequence cell.]

2. A cancellation delay could mean another charge — about 2 minutes

This Subscription Management example uses C1 detailed grounding. The user is considering whether to authorise the bank to manage a cancellation.

The baseline focuses on keeping approval with the user and having usage information to make the decision confidently. The grounded answer raises a different concern: what happens if the provider delays or the bank makes an error? Could the user be charged for another month?

The specific improvement here is user consequence. Claude increases that score from two to three.

The added point is a concrete financial risk linked to a condition: a delayed or unsuccessful cancellation. That gives us a clearer explanation of why follow-through matters to this user.

Specificity and product insight stay at three in Claude’s scores. GPT also stays at three on all three criteria. So this example supports a user-consequence improvement under Claude, rather than an increase across every measure.

For the product team, it raises a focused concern about responsibility during the pending period and the ability to follow up if processing is delayed. The answer does not establish that another charge will occur; it identifies the risk the user wants addressed.

[Pause on the highlighted question about another month’s charge and the user-consequence score.]

3. Recover the payment that already happened — about 2 minutes

This example concerns a subscription renewing when the user had meant to reconsider it. It uses C2 brief grounding.

The baseline asks for usage information, easier cancellation and less effort. The grounded answer adds refund eligibility: what exactly did I pay for, is it refundable, and how can I stop it renewing again?

Claude increases specificity from two to three and product insight from two to three.

The specificity improvement reflects the exact information needed immediately after the renewal. The product-insight improvement reflects a clearer post-renewal need: make refund eligibility and the next steps accessible without requiring the user to search for them.

The distinction is useful. Preventing the next renewal and recovering the payment that already happened are two different needs. A product could help with cancellation while still leaving the current financial concern unanswered.

Claude’s user-consequence score remains two, and GPT gives three to both versions on all three criteria. The recorded gains are specifically in specificity and product insight under Claude.

This is a straightforward example to narrate because the extra point is easy to locate in the answer: can the user get their money back?

[Pause on refund eligibility and the two green Claude cells.]

4. Pause the service to test its value — about 2 minutes

This Subscription Management example uses C4 brief grounding. The question asks what would make the product useful compared with managing subscriptions manually.

The baseline asks for more information, including usage statistics and reminders about subscription benefits. The grounded answer adds a different approach: temporarily pause a service to see whether the user misses it.

Again, Claude increases specificity from two to three and product insight from two to three.

The specificity gain comes from defining the purpose of the pause. It is a temporary test of value, rather than a general request for flexibility.

The product-insight gain comes from identifying a testable option: could a reversible pause help someone decide whether a subscription is worth keeping?

This adds a possible way to make a decision, rather than only asking for more data. It would need investigation because we have not established that the relevant provider supports pausing.

Claude’s user-consequence score stays at two. GPT gives both answers three on all three criteria. The improvement shown here is Claude’s assessment of specificity and product insight.

If time is short, these first four examples already cover all three evaluation criteria, including one example where both judges record an improvement.

[Pause on the temporary-pause proposal and its stated test purpose.]

5. Add context before sharing a summary — about 2 minutes

The fifth example returns to Mental Wellness and C2 detailed grounding. The question concerns using an app-generated summary to explain how the user has been doing.

The baseline already recognises that a recipient could misunderstand the labels or draw conclusions without hearing the user’s perspective. The grounded answer adds a concrete control: review the summary and add explanatory notes before sharing it.

Claude increases user consequence from two to three. The explanation is that willingness to share depends on being able to clarify what the labels mean. Without that control, the user prefers to speak for themselves because of the risk of misinterpretation.

This makes an existing concern more actionable. The baseline notices a communication risk; the grounded answer identifies a condition under which the user would feel more comfortable sharing.

Specificity and product insight stay at three in Claude’s scores. GPT also gives both answers three on all three criteria. So the measured improvement is specifically Claude’s user-consequence score.

The added point is clear in the highlighted text: let the user add their own context before another person reads the summary.

Close — about 1–2 minutes

Across these five examples, we can identify the improved parameters precisely. Grounding adds specificity through more exact requirements, product insight through clearer design choices, and user consequence through explicit effects on money, disclosure or willingness to share.

The broader results give these examples context. For Subscription Management, Claude rates seven of eight grounded combinations above baseline on both specificity and product insight. For Mental Wellness, user-consequence averages improve in seven of eight combinations under Claude and all eight under GPT. Those broader counts compare interview averages across seven answers per condition.

The strongest shared Wellness combination is C2 detailed. The journey graphs show the saved emotional reference used for grounding, with an explanation below each graph. Those graphs are separate from the judges’ quality scores.

These are encouraging descriptive findings from one persona identity and one interview per condition. They identify combinations worth repeating across more interviews and personas.

My main conclusion is that we can now point to a specific added contribution and the exact criterion that improved. The gains include an upfront explanation before disclosure, a financial risk during cancellation, refund eligibility, a temporary pause to assess value, and control over how a summary is interpreted.
